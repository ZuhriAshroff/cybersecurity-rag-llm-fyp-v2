Title: Ransomware Attacks — Mechanisms, Impact, and Defence
Source: CISA Ransomware Guide / NIST SP 1800-25

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts a victim's files or locks access to systems and demands payment — typically in cryptocurrency — in exchange for a decryption key. It has evolved from a nuisance into one of the most financially devastating cyber threats facing organisations, governments, and critical infrastructure worldwide.

How Ransomware Works
Ransomware typically enters an environment through phishing emails containing malicious attachments or links, exploitation of unpatched vulnerabilities, compromised remote desktop protocol (RDP) credentials, or drive-by downloads from infected websites. Once executed, it performs several steps:

1. Reconnaissance: Maps network shares, backup locations, and connected drives.
2. Privilege escalation: Attempts to gain administrator or domain-level access.
3. Lateral movement: Spreads to other hosts to maximise impact.
4. Exfiltration (double extortion): Steals sensitive data before encrypting to use as additional leverage.
5. Encryption: Uses symmetric encryption (AES-256) with the key protected by the attacker's asymmetric key (RSA-2048).
6. Ransom note: Drops instructions in each encrypted directory.

Notable Ransomware Families
WannaCry (2017) exploited the EternalBlue vulnerability in Windows SMB, infecting over 200,000 systems in 150 countries. NotPetya disguised itself as ransomware but was a destructive wiper targeting Ukrainian infrastructure. REvil and Conti operated as Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) platforms, allowing affiliates to deploy ransomware in exchange for a revenue cut. The Colonial Pipeline attack (2021) demonstrated ransomware's ability to disrupt critical physical infrastructure.

Defence Strategies
Offline backups following the 3-2-1 rule (three copies, two media types, one offsite) are the most effective recovery mechanism. Organisations should:

- Patch operating systems and software promptly, especially internet-facing services.
- Disable RDP where not required; use Network Level Authentication and MFA where it is.
- Segment networks so ransomware cannot move laterally between zones.
- Deploy endpoint detection and response (EDR) tools with behavioural analysis.
- Conduct phishing simulation training for all staff.
- Maintain and regularly test an incident response plan covering ransomware scenarios.
- Use application whitelisting to prevent unauthorised executables from running.

Payment Considerations
Security agencies including CISA and the FBI advise against paying ransoms. Payment does not guarantee decryption, funds criminal organisations, and can expose victims to sanctions violations if the attacker is a designated entity. Even after payment, recovery time averages 21 days due to remediation complexity.

Ransomware-as-a-Service
The RaaS model has professionalised ransomware operations. Developers maintain the malware and negotiation infrastructure; affiliates carry out intrusions and receive 70-80% of ransom proceeds. This division of labour has lowered the technical barrier to entry and dramatically increased attack frequency.

Regulatory Context
Many jurisdictions now require organisations to report ransomware incidents within 72 hours (GDPR) or 24-72 hours (US critical infrastructure sectors under CIRCIA). Failure to report can result in fines that exceed the ransom itself.
